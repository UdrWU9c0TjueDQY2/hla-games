Tuesday, the restaurant

A composite scenario, not a customer story.

The bill is 86.40 for four people and everyone is looking at Dev, because Dev is "good at numbers", which is a thing people say about anyone who once did it quickly. Dev is not good at numbers. Dev is good at avoiding this moment, and tonight it has arrived.

What Dev does, having read the trick that morning, is not divide 86.40 by 4. It is: 80 split four ways is 20, 6.40 split four ways is 1.60, so 21.60 each. Two steps, both easy, said out loud slowly enough to look casual. Nobody notices the trick. That is the point of the trick.

Later, on the bus, a Sumbriska board asks for three bridges into an island marked 26, with 12 and 16 on offer across the water and a 7 and a 4 on the islands behind them. Dev finds it in under a minute, with no one watching, which is how the restaurant was won.

Twelve questions, answered

Why am I so bad at mental math?

Usually because nobody taught you the tricks, not because something is wrong with you. School mostly taught column methods on paper, which are the worst methods to run in your head. Mental arithmetic is a small set of habits: make ten, round and adjust, work left to right. The calculator above shows the first one on any sum you type.

Is it too late to get better at mental math as an adult?

No. Adults who practise short, regular sessions improve, and the improvement is mostly in strategy rather than memory. National Numeracy, a UK charity, runs a free adult challenge built on exactly that idea: small steps, no shame, no timer.

Could I have dyscalculia?

Possibly, and only an assessment can say. Dyscalculia is a specific difficulty with number sense that is distinct from maths anxiety or gaps in teaching; the British Dyslexia Association has a plain description and routes to assessment. Being bad at times tables is not, on its own, dyscalculia.

Why do I freeze when someone asks me to split a bill?

That is maths anxiety, and it is common: the pressure of being watched eats the working memory you need for the sum. The fix is not more pressure. It is doing sums where nobody is watching until the strategies are automatic, which is why a quiet puzzle beats a timed quiz for most adults.

What is the make-ten trick?

Break one number so the other reaches a round ten, then add what is left. 47 + 38: take 3 from 38 to make 47 into 50, then add the remaining 35: 85. It works because tens are easy to hold in your head and the leftover is small. Type any sum above to see it.

Do number puzzle games actually help?

They help with the part most adults are missing: a relaxed place to practise seeing numbers as parts of other numbers. A Sumbriska board asks you to flip bridges so each island's incoming numbers add to its target, which is make-ten and its cousins over and over, with no clock. I was told I am "just not a maths person". Is that a thing?

It is a thing people say. It is not a finding. Most of the adults who say it had one bad year with one bad teacher and have been avoiding numbers since. Avoidance is the only part that is real, and it is the part that can change.

How long should I practise?

Ten minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday. One Daily board, or one sum with the calculator while the kettle boils. Stop while it still feels easy.

Which mental math skills matter most for everyday life?

Adding and subtracting two-digit numbers, finding 10% and halving it, and rough multiplication by rounding. Those three cover bills, discounts, cooking and "is this deal real". Nobody needs 17 x 23 in their head.

Should I use a calculator or try in my head?

Both. Use the calculator for the answer and your head for the estimate first; if they disagree by a lot, one of you typed it wrong. Estimating is the skill, and it is the one this page is for.
