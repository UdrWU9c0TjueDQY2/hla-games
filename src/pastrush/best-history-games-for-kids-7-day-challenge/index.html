This is the skill PastRush is built around: not the date, but what a source can and cannot show. Every exhibit in the app carries a label: made at the time, written later, scientific finding, modern reconstruction.

One week, made up but doable

SaturdayThe Rosetta Stone. Your ten-year-old reads that the same decree is written three ways and asks why anyone would bother. That question is the whole case. Twenty minutes, one star, and a bookmark in the Notebook.

MondayPompeii. The source-comparison round asks which of two documents supports a claim about the date; the answer is “neither”, and the child is annoyed and then pleased. You learn the eruption date is itself debated in the app's framing, which is why it is called The Disputed Day.

WednesdayÖtzi. The exhibit labels do their job: a photo of the copper axe is “made at the time”, the picture of him walking is marked an illustration. Your child points this out to you, which is the point.

FridayThe Norse in Vinland. You open the case on the globe and drag the Time River back a thousand years. The Time Thread that connects it to Pompeii is about ways of knowing, not dates.

What it looks like

How a case works, in three steps

1

Read the exhibits

Each is labelled: made at the time, written later, scientific finding, modern reconstruction. Bookmark any to the Notebook.

2

Do the historian's jobs

Order the events (BC/AD handled correctly), compare sources, link causes to effects, match time and place.

3

Decide what can be concluded

Sort claims into supported and going too far. Every answer explains why and links to the source behind it.

Questions parents ask

What age is PastRush for?

The App Store rating is 9+. The six free cases (Rosetta Stone, Pompeii, Grand Canyon rock layers, Ötzi, continental drift, the Norse in Vinland) read well with a parent beside a nine-year-old and alone from about eleven; the evidence-sorting tasks are closer to what middle-school history asks for.

Is it actually free?

Six of the twelve investigations are free for everyone, along with the globe, Notebook, Daily Evidence Check, Album and the picture games, per the listing. Founder Lifetime is a one-time purchase for cases 7 to 12 and the Master tier; the listing says no subscription and no recurring charges.

Does it need Wi-Fi?

Cases, the Notebook, stories, the Album and ancient-Earth maps work offline. The satellite map, Dive's satellite views, Where in the World? and nearby places need a connection, per the listing.

Are the pictures real?

The listing is unusually direct about this: every exhibit is labelled as made at the time, written later, a scientific finding or a modern reconstruction, and story pictures are AI-assisted illustrations marked as illustrations, never presented as photographs of real objects. Satellite imagery is from Apple Maps.

Can a classroom use it?

Class mode hides purchases and outside links on shared devices; it does not open paid content. No account, no ads, no tracking. Founder Lifetime works with Apple Family Sharing, with progress kept on each device.

Which of the other games should we pick?

For a four-year-old who likes the sea, Deep Blue (subscription). For a teenager on a commute, Paladin's lesson-and-quiz format. For pub-quiz dates, Eventology, though it has not been updated since 2019. For evidence and reasoning rather than recall, ours; that is the whole design difference, and the table shows what each listing claims.
