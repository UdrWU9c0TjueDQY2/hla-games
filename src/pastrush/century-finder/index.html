The rule

Count the first hundred.

The 1st century CE runs from 1 CE to 100 CE. The 2nd runs from 101 to 200. For a positive year number, divide by 100 and round up: 1900 ÷ 100 = 19, while 1901 ÷ 100 = 19.01, rounded up to 20.

For BCE, the century number uses the same calculation, but time moves toward smaller year numbers. The 2nd century BCE runs from 200 BCE through 101 BCE. Then the 1st century BCE runs from 100 BCE through 1 BCE.

There is no year zero in this historical BCE/CE convention: 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE. Astronomical year numbering is a different system and is not used here.

What this tool can tell you

It places an exact year into an ordinal century. It does not determine whether a historical event’s date is certain, resolve a disputed chronology or convert between calendars. If a source says “circa 200 BCE,” preserve that uncertainty rather than turning it into a precise date.

Across the era boundary

Years between BCE and CE dates

Compare the starts of two numbered years. In this historical convention, 1 BCE is followed immediately by 1 CE: the gap is one year, not two. There is no year zero.

Whole years 1–9999 only. This is a year-number comparison, not an exact elapsed-time calculator for events with unknown months or days.

Why subtract one across BCE and CE?

Walk through the boundary: 2 BCE → 1 BCE → 1 CE → 2 CE. Those four labels have three one-year gaps between them. For dates on opposite sides, add the two positive year numbers and subtract one. For dates in the same era, subtract the smaller year number from the larger.

For example, the starts of 10 BCE and 10 CE are 19 years apart. Reversing A and B keeps that distance, but changes which date is earlier. If both entries name the same year in the same era, the year-start gap is zero.

Keep the precision of your source

A source giving only a year does not tell you the month or day. Two events labelled 100 CE and 101 CE could be days apart or nearly two years apart. Use this tool to understand the year labels; do not turn them into an exact event duration. Preserve words such as “about,” “before” and “after” when a source uses them.
